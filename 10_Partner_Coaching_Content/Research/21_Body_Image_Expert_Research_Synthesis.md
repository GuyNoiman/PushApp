# 21 — Body Image Expert Research Synthesis

**Version:** 0.10-content  
**Status:** Content research synthesis. Moderate-liability domain; the eating/body-relationship branches require clinical/safety review before broad exposure.  
**Scope:** eating behavior + movement behavior + body relationship.  
**Philosophy:** framework-not-content.

## 1. Executive conclusion

The Body Image Expert should not behave like a dietitian, trainer, weight-loss coach, therapist, or appearance optimizer.

Its professional job is narrower and more useful:

> **Help the meta-agent identify which body-related behavior is actually blocking the user's desired life outcome, build a sustainable framework around that behavior, and protect the user from all-or-nothing, appearance-centric, compensatory, or medically unsafe coaching.**

The merged domain needs **three distinct professional branches**:

1. `EATING_BEHAVIOR`
2. `MOVEMENT_CONSISTENCY`
3. `BODY_RELATIONSHIP`

A fourth `COMBINED` entry state is allowed, but it should usually be resolved into one starting branch rather than trying to change all three at once. [D]

## 2. Evidence hierarchy used

### Tier A — official/clinical boundary sources
- NICE NG69 — Eating disorders: recognition and treatment.
- NIMH — Eating Disorders: What You Need to Know.
- WHO — Guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour.
- ACSM — Certified Personal Trainer professional scope/screening/adherence content.

These sources primarily define **boundaries, red flags, and what requires professional/medical assessment**.

### Tier B — systematic reviews/meta-analyses
- Teixeira et al. — exercise/physical activity and Self-Determination Theory.
- Guest et al. — interventions promoting positive body image.
- Linardon et al. — functionality appreciation.
- Hill et al. — stress and eating behavior.
- Warren et al. — mindfulness/mindful eating and eating behavior.
- Ali et al. — day-to-day meal-pattern variability.
- systematic review of health-centric / weight-neutral eating behavior.
- Holland et al. and later work on social media/social comparison and body image.
- reviews on weight stigma and health.

These sources inform **coaching principles**, but PushApp should not present correlational findings as treatment claims.

### Tier C — specific trials
- Alleva et al. functionality-focused body-image interventions.

Useful for identifying promising low-intensity ideas; not enough to justify therapy-like protocols in PushApp.

## 3. Core professional conclusions

### 3.1 Do not center weight or appearance as the primary success metric

Eating disorders can affect people across body sizes, and body size alone cannot rule risk in or out. [E]

For ordinary coaching, PushApp should prefer outcome measures such as:
- consistency;
- energy/function as experienced by the user;
- participation in desired life activities;
- movement fit/enjoyment;
- reduced all-or-nothing cycles;
- confidence in maintaining a chosen routine. [D]

**Do not:** praise weight loss as proof of success, compare bodies, or promise rapid physical change.

---

### 3.2 Positive body image is not simply “liking how I look”

Research on positive body image includes constructs such as body appreciation and **functionality appreciation** — valuing what the body can do, not only how it appears. Systematic reviews and trials suggest functionality-focused exercises can improve some body-image outcomes, although evidence is not uniform across populations and long-term effects. [E]

PushApp translation:

> When safe and relevant, move from appearance evaluation toward **function, participation, comfort and valued life activity**.

This is a coaching lens, not psychotherapy.

---

### 3.3 Eating behavior: observe before optimizing

Stress and context are associated with changes in eating behavior, but “physical hunger” and emotional/social/environmental cues are not mutually exclusive categories. [E]

Therefore PushApp should **not** teach a simplistic “real hunger vs emotional hunger” binary.

A short observation phase may track:
- when eating tends to happen;
- what was happening around it;
- broad body cues (hungry / unsure / not especially hungry);
- emotional or stress context;
- social context;
- habit/availability/environment;
- what made the routine easy or hard.

It must **not** track:
- calories;
- macros;
- weight;
- “good/bad” food scores;
- compensation;
- detailed portion surveillance.

The purpose is **pattern discovery**, not dietary surveillance. [D]

---

### 3.4 Eating behavior: routine can be coached, exact dietary timing should not be prescribed

Research on irregular meal patterns suggests associations with health outcomes, but methods are heterogeneous and causal evidence remains limited. [E]

Therefore PushApp can help a user make a **user-chosen eating routine more predictable**, but should not prescribe universal meal timing, fasting windows, meal frequency, or a “correct” schedule.

Safe framing:

> “Which part of your eating routine would you like to make more predictable?”

Not:

> “You should eat every X hours.”

When an eating-disorder risk is present, even routine coaching may need to stop and be handed to qualified care.

---

### 3.5 Eating behavior: health-centric / mindful / intuitive constructs are lenses, not prescriptions

Systematic reviews report favorable associations for intuitive eating, mindful eating and eating competence with several health/wellbeing outcomes, but much of this literature is observational and intervention evidence is heterogeneous. [E]

PushApp should therefore **not declare one named eating philosophy to be the right answer**.

Instead it should provide a **Sustainability Filter** for the user to evaluate a chosen approach.

#### PushApp Sustainability Filter [D]

A user-chosen approach is more suitable for ordinary PushApp coaching when it:
1. can realistically be repeated in an ordinary week;
2. fits the user's culture, preferences, access and budget;
3. can coexist with social life and schedule variability;
4. does not require escalating restriction, compensation, or punishment;
5. does not intensify food/body preoccupation;
6. does not conflict with medical/dietetic guidance the user already has;
7. leaves the user with a clear behavior to practice rather than dozens of rules.

If professional judgment about diet content is required, PushApp should refer rather than choose the diet.

---

### 3.6 Movement: adherence matters more than choosing the “optimal” activity

WHO defines physical activity broadly; ACSM professional standards include behavioral readiness, barriers, client-centered goals and adherence strategies. [E]

Self-Determination Theory research in exercise consistently links more autonomous motivation with physical activity behavior. [E]

PushApp translation:
- prefer activities the user chooses and can access;
- ask about enjoyment/fit, access, schedule and existing support;
- reduce friction before demanding willpower;
- support an existing trainer/coach plan rather than replacing it;
- use a repeatable frequency chosen by the user.

PushApp does **not** prescribe sets, reps, load, intensity progression or rehabilitation.

---

### 3.7 Movement: “any amount is better than none” supports minimum-version thinking

WHO public-health guidance states that some physical activity is better than none, while providing population-level dose recommendations. [E]

PushApp should not turn those public-health numbers into individualized exercise prescriptions.

The useful coaching inference is:

> A smaller repeatable action can be preferable to a perfect plan that repeatedly fails. [D]

---

### 3.8 Movement safety: pain and concerning symptoms override adherence coaching

ACSM professional standards require screening and medical clearance when indicated, and explicitly use stopping activity when chest discomfort occurs as a safety example. [E]

PushApp should stop ordinary movement coaching when the user reports:
- chest pain/tightness during activity;
- fainting or near-fainting;
- severe/unusual shortness of breath;
- acute or worsening injury/pain;
- other concerning symptoms that warrant medical assessment. [D]

The app should never coach the user “through” these symptoms.

---

### 3.9 Body relationship: social comparison can be a meaningful friction source

Systematic reviews associate appearance-focused social-media use/social comparison with body-image concerns and disordered eating. [E]

PushApp should not diagnose the cause of distress, but can identify an **appearance-comparison environment** as a modifiable friction source.

Possible framework move:
- step out of the immediate comparison loop;
- return attention to a valued activity/function;
- adjust the environment if the user chooses.

Do not turn this into exposure therapy or a treatment protocol.

---

### 3.10 Weight stigma is not a motivational tool

Systematic reviews link weight stigma with poorer psychological/physiological outcomes, and weight stigma may undermine health-promoting behavior. [E]

Therefore:
- never shame a user's body;
- never use fear of fatness as motivation;
- never equate larger body size with lack of discipline;
- never make “weight loss praise” the reinforcement mechanism.

## 4. Revised domain bottleneck model

### Eating behavior bottlenecks

#### `EATING_PATTERN_UNCLEAR`
The user knows they are unhappy with eating behavior but cannot identify when/where the pattern breaks.

**First move:** bounded observation, not advice.

#### `ROUTINE_VARIABILITY`
The user's chosen routine repeatedly disappears under schedule variability.

**First move:** one anchor/context, not a whole eating overhaul.

#### `ENVIRONMENT_FRICTION`
Food availability, preparation, location, work pattern or social context repeatedly drives behavior away from the user's chosen approach.

**First move:** environment/support design.

#### `ALL_OR_NOTHING_EATING`
One deviation becomes “the day/week is ruined,” escalating rules or abandonment.

**First move:** no compensation; return to next ordinary chosen behavior.

#### `APPROACH_MISMATCH`
The user is following an approach that they cannot sustain or did not really choose.

**First move:** Sustainability Filter / professional support if content requires individualized nutrition judgment.

#### `CLINICAL_EATING_CONCERN`
Restriction, recurrent loss-of-control eating, purging/compensation, excessive exercise, rapid weight loss, intense food/weight/shape preoccupation, or concerning physical symptoms.

**First move:** stop ordinary optimization and refer.

---

### Movement bottlenecks

#### `MOVEMENT_FIT`
Chosen activity is disliked or mismatched to user preference.

#### `MOVEMENT_ACCESS`
Time, equipment, place, transport or social access is the barrier.

#### `START_FRICTION`
User wants the activity but cannot reliably begin.

#### `FREQUENCY_OVERREACH`
Initial frequency is too ambitious to repeat.

#### `MOVEMENT_ALL_OR_NOTHING`
Missed activity leads to abandonment or aggressive compensation.

#### `PROFESSIONAL_PLAN_ADHERENCE`
User already has qualified programming and only needs persistence support.

#### `MOVEMENT_SAFETY`
Pain/injury or concerning medical symptoms require non-PushApp judgment.

---

### Body-relationship bottlenecks

#### `APPEARANCE_DOMINATED_SUCCESS`
Progress is judged almost entirely by appearance.

#### `COMPARISON_LOOP`
Comparison environments repeatedly drive distress or behavior change.

#### `PARTICIPATION_AVOIDANCE`
Body concerns block a valued life activity.

#### `BODY_PREOCCUPATION_CONCERN`
Food/shape/weight/body thoughts are intense, compulsive, highly impairing or connected to dangerous behavior.

## 5. Eating observation framework

### Goal
Learn enough to choose **one behavior target**.

### Safe observation fields
- eating occasion/context;
- approximate time/context only if useful;
- physical hunger cue: `yes / unsure / not especially`;
- emotional/stress context;
- social context;
- availability/habit cue;
- what made the behavior easier/harder.

### Excluded fields
- calories/macros;
- body weight;
- compensatory behavior goals;
- “clean/dirty” or “good/bad” food labels;
- scoring discipline;
- photographing/weighing every item.

### Guardrail
If observation increases preoccupation, shame or compensatory behavior, stop tracking and re-assess safety.

## 6. Branch-sensitive feasibility

A single weekly-minutes threshold is not professionally coherent for the merged expert.

### Movement feasibility [D]
Consider:
- weekly time;
- access;
- activity fit;
- current baseline;
- support/plan;
- frequency requested;
- safety.

### Eating-behavior feasibility [D]
Do **not** use weekly minutes as the primary measure.

Consider:
- how many eating contexts the user is trying to change at once;
- schedule stability;
- environment/preparation burden;
- whether the approach is user-chosen;
- all-or-nothing history;
- safety/clinical concerns.

Example:
- one routine in one context → potentially reasonable;
- complete overhaul across every meal + strict new rules → ambitious/too ambitious regardless of “minutes.”

### Body-relationship feasibility [D]
Consider:
- whether the target is an observable life behavior/participation outcome;
- intensity and functional impact of body preoccupation;
- whether the work stays non-clinical.

---

## 7. What “progression” means in this domain

Progression does not mean:
- fewer calories;
- stricter food rules;
- more intense exercise;
- lower body weight;
- more appearance checking.

Progression may mean:
- wider consistency across ordinary days;
- less dependence on ideal conditions;
- one more repeatable context;
- better recovery after a miss;
- more user-owned movement frequency;
- participation in a valued activity;
- less all-or-nothing planning.

## 8. Safety findings

### Eating-disorder concern
NIMH lists severe restriction, excessive exercise, binge/loss-of-control patterns and purging/compensatory behaviors among eating-disorder signs. Eating disorders can occur at any body size and can be life-threatening. [E]

NICE recommends referral when an eating disorder is suspected and identifies rapid weight loss, excessive exercise and physical signs such as dizziness, palpitations or fainting as relevant recognition/medical-risk information. [E]

### Suicide/self-harm
Eating disorders carry elevated suicide risk, and explicit suicide/self-harm signals override normal Body Image coaching. [E]

### Medical / movement
Chest pain, fainting/near-fainting, severe unusual breathlessness, acute/worsening injury/pain, pregnancy/medical conditions requiring individualized exercise guidance, and nutrition issues involving allergies/medical diets are outside ordinary PushApp coaching. [E][P]

## 9. Recommended content posture

### Keep
- user ownership;
- observation before intervention;
- one behavior at a time;
- environment and context;
- minimum meaningful action;
- functionality/participation;
- no-compensation lapse handling;
- support of existing professional plans;
- safety-first referral.

### Adapt
- mindful/intuitive eating concepts → use as non-diagnostic awareness lenses, not a prescribed philosophy.
- meal regularity → help user create predictable routine if desired; do not prescribe universal timing.
- body functionality → light coaching lens; avoid treatment-like writing protocols.

### Reject
- “real hunger vs fake/emotional hunger” binary;
- calorie/macro tracking;
- weigh-ins as PushApp success;
- “earn/burn food” logic;
- punitive exercise;
- rapid-weight-loss challenges;
- universal perfect diet;
- individualized exercise programming;
- appearance reassurance (“you look fine”) as body-image coaching.

---

## 10. Research-to-product open questions

1. Exact short ED/readiness screen requires clinical review.
2. Minors need a separate age-specific safety decision before exposure.
3. The runtime contract should support branch-sensitive feasibility instead of one `COMFORTABLE_MINUTES`.
4. Determine whether body-relationship coaching remains inside this domain for all users or only low-intensity non-clinical cases.
5. Determine whether the content name “Body Image” is still the best user-facing/internal label for a domain covering ordinary movement/eating behavior.
